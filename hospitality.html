Independent Hotel Show Miami 2026

A reflection on how connected systems, guest information, and thoughtful technology can improve hotel operations while protecting the human side of hospitality.

September 16–17, 2026Miami Beach Convention CenterMiami Beach, Florida

Introduction

The Independent Hotel Show Miami 2026 was scheduled for September 16–17, 2026, at the Miami Beach Convention Center in Miami Beach, Florida. The event focuses on luxury, boutique, and independent properties and brings together hospitality professionals, technology providers, designers, operators, and other industry guests. It is designed to support independent hoteliers through education, networking, and exposure to products and services.

I chose this event because I am interested in how businesses such as hotels and restaurants use technology to improve operations and customer service. I was especially interested in how information is collected, organized, and used throughout a guest’s stay—from the lobby and front desk to the restaurant and other hotel services.

The session that stood out to me was “Beyond the Tech Stack, Real-World Strategies for Unified Hospitality.” Its focus on connecting hospitality systems matched my interest in the relationship between information, technology, and real-world service.

Event Content and Main Ideas

The Independent Hotel Show was about more than hotel exhibits and hospitality products. It highlighted the importance of people, processes, information, and technology in contemporary hospitality.

In the session, Erynn Torrenga discussed the problem of fragmented hotel software. Independent hotels face a difficult balance: modernizing their operations while preserving the distinctive guest experience that makes their properties appealing. The presentation explored unified technology, vendor relationships, and ways to use guest information to create better experiences and encourage long-term loyalty.

This topic interested me because technology is often invisible to guests, even though it shapes many parts of their experience. At check-in, for example, staff may need access to reservation details, room preferences, payment information, special requests, and previous interactions. If the systems do not communicate effectively, guests may have to repeat information or wait while employees search across different programs. Connected systems can help authorized staff find relevant information more efficiently and provide a smoother experience.

The same idea applies to hotel restaurants. Hospitality technology can connect restaurant reservations, room charges, loyalty information, food and beverage transactions, and guest preferences. Information is not only stored for administration or recordkeeping; it can also help businesses understand customer needs and make better operational decisions. I came away with a clearer understanding that information technology is closely connected to customer service in hospitality.

Reflection on the Speaker and Presentation

I appreciated Erynn Torrenga’s presentation because it considered how technology works in real hospitality situations rather than treating technology as a separate part of the business. The idea of a unified technology environment made me think about how many systems a hotel may use to manage reservations, front-desk requests, housekeeping, payments, restaurant services, guest communications, and revenue.

When these systems are disconnected, employees may spend valuable time transferring information or searching for details. Better integration can reduce that friction and help staff focus on guests.

My main takeaway: Technology should support hospitality, not replace it. Even a hotel with advanced digital tools still needs clear communication, attentive service, and meaningful human interaction.

This balance is especially important for boutique and luxury hotels, where personal service is often part of the brand identity. The event also broadened my understanding of hospitality by showing how food and beverage, wellness, operations, design, technology, and employee interactions all contribute to a successful property. A hotel is about much more than its rooms.

Connection to Information Studies and Technology

This event connected directly to Information Studies because many of the topics involved acquiring, organizing, storing, accessing, communicating, and using information. A modern hotel provides a practical example of this process.

When a guest books a room, shares personal details, makes a payment, speaks with staff, visits the restaurant, and later submits feedback, information is created at each interaction. Hotel systems need to manage that information responsibly and make it available to authorized employees when it is needed. This requires well-designed databases, software and communication systems, information-management practices, and cybersecurity protections.

Operational information can also support decision-making. Hotel managers may use data about occupancy, guest activity, restaurant performance, preferences, and revenue to identify patterns and evaluate business performance. When information is organized and analyzed effectively, it can become useful knowledge for planning and improving services. This was one of the clearest connections I saw between hospitality and Information Studies.

Connection to My Career Goals

This event relates to my career goals because it showed me that information and technology skills can be applied across many industries, including hospitality. Before exploring this topic, I mostly associated hotel technology with reservations and electronic check-in. The event helped me see that technology supports nearly every part of hotel operations.

I am interested in information systems and in how they can improve customer experiences. In the future, I could contribute to hospitality without necessarily working in a traditional hotel role by focusing on technology, information management, digital systems, data analysis, or business processes. Information professionals can organize information, improve digital workflows, evaluate systems, and make useful information more accessible to employees.

Overall, this report reinforced my interest in careers that connect people, information, and technology. It also reminded me that successful systems should make work easier for employees and service more seamless for customers.
